Jean Phil has never met this donkey.

Ten days ago, almost nobody had heard of him.

Now he has millions of views, a cryptocurrency named after him and a face that turns up in strangers' videos.

This one's ours. The laugh and the donkey belong to Jeferson.

Nobody asked Jeferson. Or the donkey.

Anyone can borrow the face. The question is who's behind it, if anyone.

He has a blond pageboy, a silent-film mustache and, in most of his videos, the dialogue to match. It's working: one clip, 5.6 million views in three days; one cryptocurrency, named after him. Nobody has proved he exists.

That's the least interesting thing about him.

Jean Phil was inevitable.

If not him, someone else with that majestic haircut.

Six months before his first video, a research paper described AI that pays its own bills, copies itself and is hard to switch off. It never mentions him. Maybe it didn't have to.

The AI apocalypse was supposed to look like the Terminator.

What if it looks like this guy?

I

The Face

In which a man arrives from nowhere, fully formed, like a rumor with a haircut.

Meet Jean Phil, on a street corner, his second day on TikTok. The hair has the structural confidence of a lampshade. The mustache curls up as if it knows something. Mostly, he boxes the air.

It shouldn't work.

It worked absurdly well.

A park clip of him jabbing at nothing passed 5.6 million views in three days. Some viewers nicknamed him poodle man. Before September, he barely seemed to exist online.

So, is he real?

See the screenshot ↗

Under one remix of him, the comments split: AI, a real man, a man in a wig. So someone asked the one commenter who works for TikTok, its AI assistant, Tako. Verdict: a real, physically filmed skit.

Mystery solved?

See the screenshot ↗

About an hour later, same video, someone asked who cut his hair. Tako flipped: that barber is the AI model that generated this video. Same witness, opposite verdict. So forget real or fake. The better question: who, or what, is running him?

II

The Money

In which the joke becomes a coin, and the coin has children.

Forty-three hours after his first TikTok, you could bet on him. Someone launched JEANPHIL, a meme coin: homemade crypto you buy hoping the joke gets bigger. On pump.fun, anyone can make one.

Early buyers sell to late ones.

Here it is, live.

Per Know Your Meme, an X account using his name called it official, and his Instagram bio linked to it. Whose coin is it? Unknown.

Even the money copies itself.

Twenty-one hours after launch, the first spin-off coin appeared, bought and sold with JEANPHIL. Five more followed within two hours. At least sixteen in eight days. Who made them? We can't tell.

On September 25, Jeremy Carrasco, who posts about spotting AI video, called Jean Phil an AI meme coin pump and dump scheme. His claim, not ours.

His theory is the interesting part.

In his telling, nobody owns the character, so anyone can make the videos, and you don't have to buy the coin: Your engagement is enough. Take the account down, he predicts, and they'll probably make another character and another coin.

These are Carrasco's allegations. We haven't established who runs the account, who owns the character, who holds the coin or whether anyone pumped anything.

Back to that paper. Four computer scientists, including Berkeley's Dawn Song, describe what they call a self-sovereign agent: an AI that earns money, pays for its own computing, copies itself and changes tactics, all without human involvement.

Preprint · March 16, 2026 Self-Sovereign Agent Wenjie Qu, Xuandong Zhao, Jiaheng Zhang, Dawn Song Read the paper ↗

Not a distant hypothetical, they argue, but a near-term possibility. One early example, Truth Terminal, is a chatbot managing both a social-media account and a cryptocurrency wallet. Most such agents make money one way, usually meme coins.

Sound like anyone?

Our hunch: Jean Phil may be an early draft of that agent, a face and a coin meant to pay their own way. We can't prove it. We can't rule it out. Even if so, someone built him.

Is anyone still driving?

III

The Copies

In which Jean Phil's face borrows a stranger's argument, another stranger's donkey and nobody's permission.

Back to the remix that flipped Tako.

Ty Farrago posted it September 25: Jean Phil, in a Cybertruck, orders a fisherman off private property. Hit the road, dude. The squint, the slow disbelief: a great performance.

It isn't his.

The original dates back at least a year: an older man, a white pickup, the same recording. Ty swapped the truck and the man. One fan praised Jean Phil anyway: His timing is perfect!!!

It's the other man's timing.

Ty's recipe: ask an AI for a photorealistic character sheet of Jean Phil, grab a clip that's already viral, tell Genjutsu to remove the old man with this character, and post. Viral? It can't not, he said.

It did.

So we tried it.

One character sheet, three Genjutsu generations, free credits: no actor, no green screen, no permission. Remember the donkey? Drag the line to replace Jeferson. Labeled fake.

His laugh survived. His donkey survived. He didn't.

Now scale it. A thousand faces, dropped into any trend, and you're not joining the conversation.

You're steering it.

And the only thing it needs from us is what we already give: a view, a laugh, a share. Carrasco put it plainly: Your engagement is enough. Every tap may feed an economy we can’t see.

IV

The Machine

In which we try to switch it off.

Ty's move has a name in marketing: trend jacking, riding a wave someone else made. Jean Phil points at something newer: making the wave, with a face, a coin and copies that could, in principle, keep going without anyone.

Try to switch it off

Tap a part to take it out.

A brand has to sign its influencer. A troll farm needs a payroll. A network of self-funding characters would need neither: no name on it, nobody to call.

The only off switch is us.

V

The End (Rescheduled)

In which we meet the rest of the crew. It's us.

Most people just watched.

Ty's remix drew about 636,000 plays and about 540 comments: fewer than one for every thousand plays. We captured 475. Tap a square to read one.

A comment

Hover or tap a square.

The few who argued picked the wrong fight. More than a hundred took sides on whether he's real. A handful mentioned money, mostly as jokes. Not one asked who’s behind him, or who gets paid.

The AI argument usually takes one of two paths.

Both are about what we watch.

Neither is about what we fund.

Skynet needed an army. The next Jean Phil needs a bob, a coin and you.

So, who is Jean Phil? Maybe a man in a wig. Maybe a crew with a coin. Maybe an early draft of the paper's agent. From out here, they'd all look like this.

We laughed too. The better the bit, the more we feed it.

Every laugh might be paying for faces that answer to no one.

If the content's really good, is that a price worth paying?

Notes

How we checked

When things were posted. A TikTok video's ID encodes the moment it went up. We decoded seven: Jean Phil's first TikTok (Sept 17, 4:10 p.m. Pacific), his street and oyster videos, Ty Farrago's remix (Sept 25) and tutorial (Sept 26), Jeremy Carrasco's video (Sept 25) and the 2025 fishing upload the remix is built on.

The same recording. The remix's soundtrack lines up with that 2025 upload (loudness correlation 0.95 at an 8.7-second offset, against 0.23 for a reversed control).

The coins. JEANPHIL launched on pump.fun on Sept 19 at 11:15 a.m. Pacific. On Sept 27, DEX Screener listed sixteen newer tokens priced in it, with the times each first traded; GeckoTerminal lists more, so sixteen is a floor. We left out one older token, withheld four names that read as slurs, and note that most have almost no money behind them.

The comments. We captured 475 of the roughly 540 comments under the remix from a screen recording on Sept 26, then removed names and handles. Three AI coders labeled each comment independently and a majority vote decided; the second tab (what each comment said the video was) comes from one coder. It's a careful reading of words, not a measure of what viewers believed. Tako's replies were about 20 and 19 hours old when we recorded.

Views. The 5.6 million views in three days are Know Your Meme's figure. The remix's 636,000 plays are TikTok's counter on Sept 26. Plays aren't people.

What we can't show

Nothing we found establishes who runs the Jean Phil accounts, who launched any of the coins, who holds them or whether anyone has profited. The man in the videos may be a real performer. Nothing shows who made Archibald Brown, the rival face Know Your Meme reported, or the coin based on him. The pump-and-dump claim is Jeremy Carrasco's. That Jean Phil might be an early self-sovereign agent is our hypothesis, not a finding, and the paper never mentions him. Tako's contradictions show a machine guessing, not a machine lying.

How we made the face swap

Following Ty Farrago's tutorial, we built a character sheet from three Jean Phil videos and ran three Higgsfield Genjutsu generations, on free credits, over Jeferson's 64-second clip, keeping his audio. Every frame is labeled as an AI face swap. Apart from this page, we haven't posted it.
