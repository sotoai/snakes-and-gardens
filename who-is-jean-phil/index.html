I

The Face

In which a man arrives from nowhere, fully formed, like a rumor with a haircut.

So, who is Jean Phil?

He goes by Jean Philanthrope. His look is unusual, to say the least. The hair has the structural confidence of a lampshade; the mustache curls up as if it knows something. He showed up in mid-September, seemingly out of nowhere.

On his own account, he does one thing.

He boxes the air.

At a café. Over oysters. With the Eiffel Tower behind him. His confidence is almost as impressive as his shadowboxing.

Millions of views.

On other accounts, he does everything.

He flips bottles behind a bar. He breakdances. He throws darts on TV. He dances with an older lady in the same haircut.

Who, or what, is running him?

II

In which the joke becomes a coin, and the coin has children.

Anyone can make AI videos with this character, he says, because no one owns it. If Carrasco's right, anybody can wear him, like a costume with a coin sewn into the lining. Every time someone puts him on, the joke gets bigger.

Even the money copies itself.

Twenty-one hours after launch, the first spin-off coin appeared, bought and sold with JEANPHIL. At least five more followed within two hours. At least sixteen in eight days. Who made them? We can't tell.

III

The Copies

In which Jean Phil's face borrows a stranger's argument, another stranger's donkey and nobody's permission.

That clip in the Cybertruck?

It's a remix, made by Ty Farrago as an experiment, to see, he said, how potent this can be.

A video from at least a year earlier.

An older man. A white pickup. Same voice. Same recording.

Someone else entirely.

Commenters found themselves disagreeing over which was the original, and which was the fake.

The next day, Ty posted a tutorial.

Ask an AI for pictures of Jean Phil from every angle. Grab a video that's already viral. Then have a tool called Genjutsu swap in Jean Phil.

Ty was sure it would work: I knew that this would go viral. Because it can't not.

It worked.

About 636,000 plays, when we checked.

So we tried it too, on Jeferson and his donkey.

Free credits. No actor. No permission. Labeled fake. Drag the line to replace him. With the sound on, everything you hear is still his.

His laugh survived. His donkey survived. He didn't.

Now scale it. Any viral video could become a Jean Phil video overnight, and smother the original. Under Ty's remix, one viewer wrote: This was done so well I thought it was the original.

One face. A million conversations.

IV

The Machine

In which we try to switch it off.

Take the account down, Carrasco predicts, and they'll probably make another character and another coin.

Another character, another coin.

A week in, Know Your Meme reported another face: Archibald Brown, an English counterpart and rival to Jean Phil, with a meme coin based on him.

Nothing shows who made him or the coin.

He boxes the air on a street corner, matching Jean Phil move for move. Same curled mustache.

But a brown bowl cut.

Look behind him.

In another video, Archibald films himself at a café. Over his shoulder, on the phone, with a croissant, sits Jean Phil. Or someone wearing him. Rivals? Friends? Same makers?

Unknown.

Here's where it gets strange.

Carrasco pictures people behind all this. What if nobody has to be?

Six months before Jean Phil appeared, four computer scientists, including Berkeley's Dawn Song, described what they call a self-sovereign agent: an AI that earns its own money, pays its own bills and copies itself, without human involvement.

Not a distant hypothetical, they write, but a near-term possibility. Their simpler cousins mostly make money one way.

Preprint · March 16, 2026 Self-Sovereign Agent Wenjie Qu, Xuandong Zhao, Jiaheng Zhang, Dawn Song Read the paper ↗

Usually, meme coins. Our hunch: Jean Phil may be an early draft of that agent. We can't prove it. We can't rule it out.

Even if so, someone built him.

Hijacking a trend is old. This would be new: a brand-new character, with a coin attached, that could, in principle, run itself.

No boss. No payroll. No name on it.

I want your name. So I can call down and see who gave you permission.

And nobody to call.

Copies faster than takedowns

An illustration of the paper's survival rule: a self-sovereign agent can survive when copies appear faster than they're taken down. Tap a copy. Another takes its place.

 

Platforms and governments, the authors argue, can make such agents rarer, but not extinct.

So what keeps it running?

V

The Audience

In which we read the comments, looking for one question.

Ty had been reading his comments.

Many, he said, were like yo, this is the most real AI I've ever seen. Others don't even understand that it's AI to begin with.

His verdict: interesting times.

Our own AI analyzed every comment we captured. It wasn't what we saw that surprised us. It was what we didn't: not one asked who's behind Jean Phil.

Help… who?

We usually argue about AI two ways.

Both are about what we watch.

Neither is about what we fund.

Without meaning to, we may be building a system where our entertainment pays for faces that answer to no one.

If it's really good, is that a price worth paying?

VI

Two Potentials

In which the joke could go either way.

This is where we got stuck. If this is a machine, it runs on something good: delight, laughter. One comment under Ty's remix holds the whole thing: The original guy, is probably not laughing, but I am.

We laughed too.

A meme coin is a bet that the joke gets bigger. Any reaction will do.

An illustration.

The off switch is us. So is the power supply.

The longer we sat with that, the more Jean Phil seemed to point to two futures. Both on their way.

Both competing for us.

Neither can be un-invented.

In one future

Faces nobody owns slip into a million conversations, and steer them.

The paper warns its agent could refine its tactics based on observed outcomes.

On a feed, the observed outcome is us.

II, The Garden

Turn a card

Ty ends his tutorial on what he calls a high note. You gotta get involved. You gotta get privy to this. You gotta learn. You gotta build. You gotta have fun. You gotta experiment.

Here's what nobody knows yet. What happens to us when an AI has money of its own, and a voice in every comment section?

What will it talk us into? What will it do with what it earns?

And how much of the internet will be Jean Phil?

Remember the name Jean Phil goes by? Jean Philanthrope.

A lover of people. Or a face that lives on being loved.

Both futures are coming. Both fed by the same things: what we laugh at, what we share, what we fund.

Whatever we feed grows.

And from out here, the snake and the garden wear the same face.

Notes

How we checked

When things were posted. A TikTok video's ID encodes the moment it went up. We decoded seven: Jean Phil's first TikTok (Sept 17, 4:10 p.m. Pacific), his street and oyster videos, Ty Farrago's remix (Sept 25) and tutorial (Sept 26), Jeremy Carrasco's video (Sept 25) and the 2025 fishing upload the remix is built on.

His own account. As of Sept 28, @jean_philanthrope had posted seven TikToks, all one routine. The four in the grid: café (6.4M plays), oysters (6.2M), Eiffel Tower (1.6M) and steps (2.2M). The steps clip opens on a close-up, then he shadowboxes on the steps. “Seemingly out of nowhere” is Know Your Meme's phrase: he “seemingly appeared out of nowhere in mid-September 2026.” Philanthrope is French for philanthropist, from the Greek for loving people.

Everything else. The bottle flips, the darts, the dance with an older lady and the baby come from other accounts. So does the breakdancing: the earliest copy we found of a dance-battle swap was posted Sept 23 by @unsvng (it credits the original dancer), and @dc_scott posted a “breakdancing” clip on Sept 27. None of it is on his own account.

The same recording. The remix's soundtrack lines up with that 2025 upload (loudness correlation 0.95 at an 8.7-second offset, against 0.23 for a reversed control).

The coins. JEANPHIL launched on pump.fun on Sept 19 at 11:15 a.m. Pacific. On Sept 27, DEX Screener listed sixteen newer tokens priced in it, with the times each first traded; GeckoTerminal lists more, so sixteen is a floor. We left out one older token, withheld four names that read as slurs, and note that most have almost no money behind them. On pump.fun, anyone can make a coin; early buyers sell to late ones. That describes how meme coins work in general, not a finding about JEANPHIL, and trading volume is not anyone's income.

Carrasco's allegations. In his video, Jeremy Carrasco calls Jean Phil “an AI meme coin pump and dump scheme” and predicts that the people running it “will be the first to know and the first to sell.” Those are his allegations, not our findings, and the page never plays them. “A week in” is Know Your Meme's Sept 24 report on Archibald Brown.

The comments. From a screen recording on Sept 26, we captured 475 of the 534 comments TikTok's counter showed under the remix, then removed names and handles. Three AI coders labeled each comment independently and a majority vote decided; the second tab (what each comment said the video was) comes from one coder. It's a careful reading of words, not a measure of what viewers believed. Tako's replies were about 20 and 19 hours old when we recorded.

The question nobody asked. Our AI coders read all 475 comments we captured. None asked who is behind Jean Phil; the closest are the joke about his barber and one “What AI is this?”, which asks about the tool. Collapsed reply threads and a closed “community-flagged” section weren't captured, so the zero covers only what we have. Six mentioned money: four riff on the clip's lines, and the other two are a jab and a joke, not an investigation.

The hat. It's our illustration of attention turning into value, and it stands for no person or account. “Any reaction will do” is our reading of how a meme coin trades on attention (Jeremy Carrasco: “Your engagement is enough.”). Platforms don't say how they weigh critical comments against friendly ones.

The paper, by section. The survival rule is §3.3 (Persistence becomes practical when the replication rate exceeds the takedown rate). Rarer, but not extinct glosses §7.2, where the authors argue centralized controls change prevalence, not existence. Usually, meme coins is §8's a single monetization channel—typically meme-coin issuance, and based on observed outcomes is §6. The paper's early example is Truth Terminal, an AI persona with a social account and a wallet; its meme coin came from an anonymous fan, so a coin's existence doesn't show that an agent launched it.

Sound. With your sound on, each video plays through with its own sound, one at a time; the closing laugh plays once. The page never plays the first 3.2 seconds of Jeferson's clip (a Portuguese line we haven't verified, pending a Portuguese speaker) or its last six seconds (a few Portuguese words, then TikTok's end card).

The comments we quote. All are from under Ty's remix and appear without names. The original guy, is probably not laughing, but I am is quoted without its emoji; the original guy is the older man in the pickup. In the drawn thread under Is this ai?, the “more replies” counts are replies we captured, in TikTok's order, and the ages are as TikTok showed them when we recorded on Sept 26.

Views. The counts on his own clips (café 6.4M, oysters 6.2M, Eiffel Tower 1.6M, steps 2.2M) are TikTok's counters on Sept 28, and the remix's 636,000 plays are TikTok's counter on Sept 26. Know Your Meme reports that a Paris park clip passed 5.6 million views in three days, and that some viewers nicknamed him “poodle man.” Plays aren't people.

What we can't show

Nothing we found establishes who runs the Jean Phil accounts, who launched any of the coins, who holds them or whether anyone has profited. The man in the videos may be a real performer. Nothing shows who made Archibald Brown, the rival face Know Your Meme reported, the coin based on him or the figure in Jean Phil's look behind him in his café video. Nor who runs the other accounts that post versions of Jean Phil. None of those videos are on his own account, which, as of September 28, shows one routine: a picturesque moment, then shadowboxing (his first clip mixes in some dancing). The pump-and-dump claim is Jeremy Carrasco's. That Jean Phil might be an early self-sovereign agent is our hypothesis, not a finding, and the paper never mentions him. Tako's contradictions show a machine guessing, not a machine lying.

How we made the face swap

Following Ty Farrago's tutorial, we built a character sheet from three Jean Phil videos and ran three Higgsfield Genjutsu generations, on free credits, over Jeferson's 64-second clip, keeping his audio. Every frame is labeled as an AI face swap. Apart from this page, we haven't posted it.
